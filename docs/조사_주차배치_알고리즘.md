# Maximum-capacity parking layout: literature and practical algorithms

## (A) Key algorithms

**A1. Grid MIP: Stephan, Weidinger & Boysen, "Layout Design of Parking Lots with Mathematical Programming", Transportation Science 55(4):930-945, 2021, doi 10.1287/trsc.2021.1049** [S1]
- Rasterizes the lot into square cells. The model maximizes the number of stalls, where each stall must be reachable by a driving lane. Three models at three grid resolutions show the trade-off between more stalls and more compute. Tested on 177 real lots in Jena.
- A later paper reports that manual layouts had about 25% fewer stalls than the MIP layouts [S2]. Per-resolution solve times are not in the sources I could read (unverified).

**A2. Branch-and-cut: Thomas & Rambha (IISc), arXiv 2506.09961, 2025** [S2]. This is the best current formulation to copy.
- Cells are 3x3 m. A stall is 2x1 cells (6x3 m), at 0° or 90°. A two-way lane is 2 cells wide.
- Variables: binary anchors for each cell: 0° stall, 90° stall, or driving field.
- Constraints:
  - Each cell has at most one use.
  - Every stall touches a driving field.
  - Driving fields connect to the entrance.
- Connectivity, three ways:
  - (a) A flow model with big-M (the baseline).
  - (b) "Hop" valid inequalities added up front, built from vertex cut sets at hop distance from the entrance.
  - (c) Lazy cuts. At each incumbent, find the driveway components not connected to the entrance and add a minimum vertex-cut inequality.
- Results:
  - Solver: CPLEX 22.1, with NetworkX for the cuts. 325 NYC lots of 6,011-28,436 sq ft (about 560-2,640 m²).
  - Median solve time with branch-and-cut: 2.6 s two-way, 5.4 s one-way. Flow model: 12.8 s and 43.2 s.
  - Within 15 minutes, the flow model solved 187 of 325 two-way instances to optimality; branch-and-cut solved 245.
  - One-way lanes gave about 18.6% more stalls.
- My own rough estimate: those lots are about 60-290 cells. A 10,000 m² floor at 1.25 m cells is about 6,400 cells and roughly 20-25k binaries. That is far larger than their instances, so solving the full grid in under 1 minute with HiGHS or CP-SAT is unlikely (my inference, unverified).

**A3. Forbes & Harris (UQ), TRISTAN XI 2022** [S3]
- Same idea, but with precomputed "tiles": a parking tile is 1x2 cells, a driving tile is 2x2 cells.
- No big-M is needed, but the LP relaxation is weak.
- Connectivity uses logic-based Benders lazy cuts on disconnected driving regions. A cheaper "flow heuristic" requires each active driving tile to have an active neighbour closer to the entrance (in BFS distance).
- The flow heuristic reached the optimum on the Stephan Fig. 1 instance in under 1 minute.
- **The flow heuristic is the easiest connectivity constraint to put into CP-SAT.** It is a strong idea for us: precompute BFS distance from the ramp on the candidate aisle cells.

**A4. Abdelfatah & Taha (2014)** [S2, citing]
- MIP solved with LINDO, for stall angles; rectangular lots only.
- I could not retrieve the original paper (unverified beyond the citation).

**A5. Module-count ILP: Zhang, Li & Yu, CAADRIA 2023 (HIT Shenzhen / XKOOL)** [S4]
- Integer counts of 20 row "modes": single or double rows at 0/30/45/60/90°. Constraints are that the sum of mode widths ≤ site width minus the perimeter driveways, and the aisles form loops with no dead ends.
- Solved with CPLEX in 300-460 ms for up to about 500 stalls.
- Area per stall: 25.5-27.6 m² on rectangles of 90x80 to 133x97 m.
- Rectangles only. This is essentially a 1D knapsack over strip widths, cheap enough to run per zone.

**A6. Rule-based generation with orientation search: Cudzik & Nessel, Sustainability 16(2):592, 2024** [S5]
- Method:
  - Perpendicular stalls along the whole perimeter, with a ring road inside them.
  - Parallel double-loaded rows in the interior.
  - Leftover edge strips filled with parallel stalls.
  - Brute force over the grid aligned to each boundary edge, plus pairs of directions; 30-54 variants per site.
- Results: gained 0 to 484 stalls over existing lots. One site went from 27.7 to 22.0 m² per stall.

**A7. Tile-and-trim and network capacity: ESGI91, Arup study group, 2013** [S6]
- Tile-and-trim: overlay the densest infinite pattern (90° double-loaded), rotate and shift it to maximize the stalls inside, then trim stalls to connect lanes and clear obstacles.
- Road-network search with a GA plus Nelder-Mead got stuck in local minima.
- Useful findings:
  - Long aisles with 90° stalls are the densest.
  - Tree-shaped networks with few bends beat loops by about 5-10%.
  - Perimeter roads should be kept at a constant offset from the walls.
  - Re-routing one perimeter road on Arup's real plan raised capacity from 202 to 220/233 (the original design had 147).

**A8. WaveFunctionCollapse: Lan, Chen & Xu, Buildings 13(11):2898, 2023** [S7]
- 77 modules and 278 adjacency rules on an 8.4 m column grid, followed by multi-objective filtering.
- About 47-90 s for 300 results.
- No stall-count benchmark is given, and the authors note that redundant driveways eat stalls. **Not recommended for us.**

**A9. Commercial tools**
- TestFit: says it searches the bay configuration and the ramp location that removes the fewest stalls, and fills irregular shapes. Internals are undocumented [S8].
- Architechtures: places a perimeter lane at an offset from the boundary first, then fills the inside [S9].
- Both match A6/A7: perimeter ring plus interior double-loaded bays.

## (B) Heuristics to implement first (ranked by stall gain per effort)

1. **Stalls on both sides of every aisle, including connector aisles and perimeter wall stalls.**
   - Effort: low.
   - Treat every aisle segment the same way: offset ±(3.0 + 5.0) m and pack 2.5 m stalls along each side. Treat walls as the back edge for perimeter rows.
   - Every source above parks along all lanes [S2, S5, S6]. Large gain on short or blocky floors.
2. **Exact residual fill with a set-packing model (CP-SAT or HiGHS) after the aisle network is fixed.** This is our version of tile-and-trim [S6].
   - Effort: medium. Gain: closes most "leftover strip / chamfer / beside ramp" losses.
   - Generate candidate stall rectangles (2.5x5.0) with shapely:
     - Along every aisle edge, at a fine phase step of 0.25-0.5 m.
     - Both perpendicular and, in leftover strips, other orientations.
   - Keep only candidates that are inside the floor, outside the cores and ramp, and whose 2.5 m short side touches an aisle.
   - Add a clique constraint per overlap group, or use NoOverlap2D in CP-SAT. Maximize the count.
   - A few thousand candidates should solve in seconds (my estimate).
3. **Per-row phase optimization.**
   - Effort: low.
   - Slide each row's stall phase independently (not one global phase) so the row starts flush against cores, the ramp and chamfers. 1D DP or greedy per row.
4. **Zone decomposition with orientation per zone.**
   - Effort: medium.
   - Split the floor along core and ramp edges and reflex vertices into near-rectangles.
   - For each zone, try angles from the edge directions [S5] and solve the module stacking as a 1D knapsack over [row|aisle|row] widths, with single rows allowed at the edges [S4].
   - Then stitch the zone aisles into a connected tree.
5. **Prefer a tree aisle network** with few bends, long aisles parallel to the long side, and single-loaded aisles kept to a minimum [S6].
   - Score each candidate network by stalls after step 2. Use the Forbes-Harris "neighbour closer to the ramp" rule as the connectivity check [S3].
6. **Full grid MIP (A2/A3)**
   - Effort: high.
   - Use it only on small residual sub-areas or as an offline benchmark on small floors. It is too slow for full 10,000 m² floors in under 1 minute (inference).
   - Note the cell-size mismatch: 2.5x5.0 m stalls with a 6.0 m aisle have no common square cell other than 0.5 m. A 1.25 m grid would turn the aisle into 6.25 m.

## (C) Benchmarks

- Pure 90° double-loaded module, my arithmetic: (5+6+5) m x 2.5 m = 40 m² per 2 stalls, which is **20 m² per stall net**. This is the theoretical floor before perimeter rings, cores, ramps and columns.
- Optimized surface lots:
  - 22.0 m² per stall [S5].
  - 22.4 m² per car with roads [S6].
  - 25.5-27.6 m² per stall on rectangles with a 6 m driveway [S4].
- Korean apartment underground parking, 11 projects since 2000 [S10]:
  - **31.1 m² per stall** where the complex also has ground parking.
  - **37.6 m² per stall** with no ground parking.
  - These are gross figures that include structure and cores.
  - The same source notes modules grew from 6.5x7.5 to 7.5x8.0 or 8.0x8.0 m.
- Korean column modules [S11]:
  - Standard stall 2.5x5.0 m; expanded stall 2.6x5.2 m. **At least 30% of non-parallel stalls must be expanded.** Our engine should model this.
  - 6-bay module (3 stalls between columns) is 8.1x8.0 m. 8-bay module is 10.7x8.0 m with 0.5 m columns.
- US: above-ground garages run 300-350 sq ft per space (about 28-33 m²); below-grade or mixed-use garages 400-500 sq ft (about 37-46 m²) [S12]. TestFit cites under 400 sq ft per stall as efficient [S8].
- **A target of about 28-33 m² per stall gross for a Korean underground floor is plausible. Below about 27 is excellent. Above 35 means stalls are being lost** (my synthesis of S10-S12).
